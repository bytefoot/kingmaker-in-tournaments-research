# P3 — Bradley–Terry swap-monotonicity

**Status (updated 22 Nov 2026 after reading the original paper).**
- **P3 is a known theorem: Chung & Hwang (1978), JASA 73(363):593–596, "Theorem" on p. 594.** For a Bradley–Terry
  preference scheme and *any* knockout plan $T_n$ (any tree shape, any $n$): $\pi_i \ge \pi_j \Rightarrow
  P_i(T_n, A) \ge P_j(T_n, A_{ij})$ for every assignment $A$, where $A_{ij}$ swaps $C_i$ and $C_j$. That is exactly our
  statement. Their proof is by induction on $n$: if some first-round game involves neither $i$ nor $j$, condition on its
  result and use the $n-1$ case; otherwise the relevant subtrees are ladder trees, handled by their Lemma 2 (via Lemma 1,
  proved in the Appendix). **Novelty must not be claimed for P3; the report should cite it.**
- **Our own proof** (below) is an independent route to the same result: a factorisation plus a "Ladder Lemma", with a
  computer-algebra certificate for ladder depth ≤ 4 (so n ≤ 32 for balanced brackets) and a hand proof for n = 4. Their
  Lemma 2 plays the role of our Ladder Lemma, and their induction covers every depth, so P3 holds for all n.
- **Corollary (i), "the weakest player is never favourite"**: an immediate consequence of their theorem (take $j$ = the
  weakest player and $i$ = its first-round opponent; swapping first-round partners gives the same bracket). Not stated
  in their paper; trivial given it.
- **Corollary (ii)**: known (Adler et al. 2017, Lemma 2; also follows from the DP).
- **Corollary (iii), K ≤ n − 1 and attained**: our observation (attainment construction below).
- **Their paper also shows BT matters**: with only strong stochastic transitivity they give a 5-player ladder-tree
  counterexample to the theorem (their Figure B), and they conjecture that the averaged corollary still holds under SST.
  Israel (1981) later disproved that conjecture.
- Numerical: 0 violations in 999,480 random tests (n = 4, 8, 16) and 2.4 M adversarial hill-climb evaluations
  (`theory/search_counterexamples.py`, `outputs/csv/p3_search.csv`), consistent with the theorem.

## Statement
BT: $P(u \text{ beats } v) = a_u/(a_u+a_v)$ with $a_u = e^{g_u} > 0$. Fix a bracket σ with player $i$ at slot $a$ and $j$
at slot $b$, and let σ′ be σ with $i$ and $j$ exchanged. If $g_i > g_j$ then $W_i(\sigma') > W_j(\sigma)$.
In words: put the stronger player in the weaker player's place, and they do strictly better than the weaker player did there.

## Literature
- Chung & Hwang (1978), *Do stronger players win more knockout tournaments?*, JASA 73(363):593–596 (read in full; PDF in
  `literature/`, not committed). Theorem = P3 for any tree; Corollary = the random-draw average. Lemma 1 (Appendix) and
  Lemma 2 cover ladder trees. Counterexamples under (strong) stochastic transitivity are on p. 595.
- Israel (1981), *Stronger players need not win more knockout tournaments*, JASA 76(376):950–951: under only strong
  stochastic transitivity, the random-draw statement can fail. So the BT structure matters.
- Adler, Cao, Karp, Peköz & Ross (2017), *Random knockout tournaments*, Oper. Res. (arXiv 1612.04448): Lemma 2 gives
  corollary (ii) by coupling. They also show that a player's win probability is **not** decreasing in other players'
  strengths for n ≥ 4, so a naive "others got stronger ⇒ I do worse" proof of P3 cannot work.
- Horen & Riezman (1985), Oper. Res. 33(2):249–262: no 8-player seeding is "fair" (stronger ⇒ higher W) on the doubly
  monotone domain. That concerns a single σ, which P3 does not claim.
- Karpov (2015), *A theory of knockout tournament seedings*, Heidelberg DP 600: survey of the seeding axioms.

## Our independent proof (the theorem itself is Chung & Hwang's)

**Notation.** For a subtree $S$ not containing $i$ or $j$, let $q_S$ be the distribution of its champion. For a player $x$
outside $S$, $E_S(x) = \sum_m q_S(m)\,P(x \text{ beats } m)$. Under BT, $P(i \text{ beats } m) > P(j \text{ beats } m)$ for
every $m \ne i, j$, so $E_S(i) > E_S(j)$ for every such $S$. (★)

**Step 1 (factorisation).** Let $v$ be the lowest common ancestor of slots $a$ and $b$, with child subtrees $L \ni b$ and
$R \ni a$. Unrolling the DP $q_v(x) = q_L(x)\sum_{m \in R} q_R(m)P(x,m)$ and then the levels above $v$: for the player
$x$ at slot $b$ with $y$ at slot $a$,
$$W_x = \underbrace{\prod_{S \in \mathcal{S}_L} E_S(x)}_{e_L(x)} \cdot G(x; y) \cdot \underbrace{\prod_{S \in \mathcal{S}_{\uparrow}} E_S(x)}_{U(x)},
\qquad G(x;y) = \sum_{m} q_R^{(y)}(m)\, P(x \text{ beats } m),$$
where $\mathcal{S}_L$ are the sibling subtrees met on the way from $b$ up to $L$'s root, $\mathcal{S}_\uparrow$ those met above
$v$, and $q_R^{(y)}$ is the champion distribution of $R$ with $y$ at $a$. No subtree in $\mathcal{S}_L \cup \mathcal{S}_\uparrow$
contains slot $a$ or $b$, so all of them are the same in σ and σ′.

So $W_i(\sigma') = e_L(i)\,G(i;j)\,U(i)$ and $W_j(\sigma) = e_L(j)\,G(j;i)\,U(j)$. By (★), $e_L(i) > e_L(j)$ and $U(i) > U(j)$
(empty products are 1). All factors are positive, so it suffices to show $G(i;j) \ge G(j;i)$.

**Step 2 (reduction to a ladder).** Let $T_1, \dots, T_h$ be the sibling subtrees met on the way from slot $a$ up to $R$'s root
($h$ = the **ladder depth**, $h \le \log_2 n - 1$), with champions $c_1, \dots, c_h$. These are independent, contain
neither $i$ nor $j$, and have the same distribution in σ and σ′. The champion of $R$ is the survivor of the ladder:
the occupant of $a$ plays $c_1$, the winner plays $c_2$, …, up to $c_h$. Conditioning on $(c_1,\dots,c_h) = (w_1,\dots,w_h)$,
$$G(x;y) = \mathbb{E}_{c}\big[\Lambda(x; y; c_1,\dots,c_h)\big],$$
where $\Lambda(x;y;w)$ is the probability that $x$ beats the survivor of the ladder started by $y$ against $w_1,\dots,w_h$.
It therefore suffices to prove:

**Ladder Lemma.** For all positive $a_x > a_y$ and $w_1,\dots,w_h > 0$: $\Lambda(x;y;w) > \Lambda(y;x;w)$.

**Step 3 (Ladder Lemma for h ≤ 4).**
- $h = 0$: $\Lambda(x;y) = \frac{x}{x+y} > \frac{y}{x+y} = \Lambda(y;x)$ (writing $x, y, z$ for the odds $a$).
- $h = 1$, by hand: multiplying by $(x+y)(y+z)(x+z) > 0$,
  $\Lambda(x;y;z) - \Lambda(y;x;z) \propto x^2(y+z) + 2xyz - y^2(x+z) - 2xyz = (x-y)\,(xy + xz + yz) > 0.$
- $h = 2, 3, 4$: `theory/ladder_lemma.py` expands $\Lambda(x;y;w) - \Lambda(y;x;w)$ over a common denominator whose
  polynomial has only positive coefficients. It checks that the numerator equals $(x-y)\cdot Q$, where $Q$ has **only
  positive coefficients** (16, 125 and 1,296 monomials). With all variables positive and $x > y$, both are positive. ∎
  (The $h = 1$ output reproduces the hand computation, $Q = xy + xz + yz$, as a check of the script.
  Separately, `ladder()` agrees with a 400,000-run simulation to within 1 s.e.)

Balanced brackets with n ≤ 32 have $h \le 4$, so P3 holds for them. For n = 4 the whole argument is by hand ($h \le 1$).

**Open (general n).** Prove the Ladder Lemma for all $h$, e.g. by induction on $h$. Peeling off the first opponent gives
$\Lambda(x;y;w) = P(y\,\text{beats}\,w_1)\,\Lambda(x;y;w') + P(w_1\,\text{beats}\,y)\,\Lambda(x;w_1;w')$. The difficulty is
that the mixing weights differ between the two sides. The monomial counts $1, 3, 16, 125, 1296 = (h+2)^h$ are
Cayley's numbers of labelled trees on $h+2$ vertices, which suggests a combinatorial (tree-counting) proof might exist.
We have not pursued it.

## Corollaries

**(ii) $W_i(\sigma)$ is strictly increasing in $g_i$.** $W_i = \prod_{\text{levels}} E_S(i)$ with all $q_S$ independent of
$g_i$, and each factor is increasing in $g_i$. ∎ (Also Adler et al. 2017, Lemma 2. Numerically 0 violations in 10⁶ tests.)

**(i) The weakest player is never the favourite, for every n.** More strongly: **in any bracket, a player whose first-round
opponent is stronger has a strictly smaller win probability than that opponent.** Proof: take $i$ = the stronger
first-round opponent and $j$ = the player. Exchanging siblings gives the same bracket, so σ′ = σ and P3 with $h = 0$
(which is fully proved) gives $W_i(\sigma) > W_j(\sigma)$. ∎ Numerically the weakest player's best margin is always ≤ −1.2e-3
(`p3_search.csv`).

**(iii) Under BT, $K \le n-1$, and this is attained.** By (i), only players who are stronger than their first-round opponent can be
the favourite; the weakest player is never one of them. The bound is attained: with $n-1$ equal players and one hopeless player, whoever
meets the hopeless player in round 1 has $W = 2^{-(\log_2 n - 1)}$, against $\le 2^{-\log_2 n}$ for everyone else. Each of the
$n-1$ can be drawn against them, and a small perturbation keeps all strengths distinct. Found numerically at n = 8 with
$g \approx (0.008, 0.001, 0.0004, 0.0003, 0.0002, -0.0001, -0.004, -5.0)$, giving K = 7 (`outputs/csv/p3_max_K_bt.csv`).
The P2 example is the n = 4 case (K = 3). So the BT "kingmaker" set can be almost everyone. What BT forbids is the **bottom
player**, and (P6, conjectured) any gap in the ranking.

## How this connects to the research question
Under BT the organiser can crown up to n − 1 players, but never the weakest. In expectation few are crownable (E1: mean K ≈ 2 of 8),
and the crownable set appears to be a top segment (P6). Cycles break P3 (P4), and with them both restrictions.
